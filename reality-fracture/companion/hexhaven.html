The format in one read

Reality Fracture is a medium-slow midrange format. Games are decided on turns 8–12, card advantage beats raw tempo, and the two aggressive pairs are preying on a table whose default is value. Two mechanics do most of the work.

Empower Jace — pricing it in a pick

Three surveils cost the same three loyalty as one card draw, so surveil is the efficient rate and drawing is the luxury rate. Price an empower clause like this, then adjust for your lane:

Text on cardWorthHow to treat it
Empower Jace 1≈ 0.15 cardsA scry on a stick. Never a reason to pick.
Empower Jace 2≈ 0.3 cardsReal, still only a tiebreaker.
Empower Jace 3≈ 0.5–0.7 cardsMeaningful. A full card if you'll live to spend it.
Empower 3 + board impactpremiumThis is what you're hunting at common.
Multiply by 1.3 in U/B and W/U — there, each surveil also fires a payoff, so you're paid twice. Multiply by 0.7 in R/W and B/R, where a deferred half-card is worse than a body that attacks now.
Spend loyalty the turn you get it if you're under pressure. Your Jace is a permanent your opponent can attack; banked loyalty on a Jace that dies is loyalty you set on fire. Bank only when your board is stable. And don't attack their Jace unless it's actively winning them the game — take the life total.

Empower never anti-synergises: you can't make a second token, so extras just stack counters. 4+ empower cards in your 40 is a genuine engine; 1–2 is incidental.

Prepared — and the timing rule that wins games

A prepared creature puts a copy of its spell in exile. The copy vanishes if the creature leaves the battlefield or becomes unprepared. Some creatures re-prepare at your upkeep — and a creature that's already prepared can't re-prepare.

Spend the copy every turn cycle on an upkeep-repreparer, even at break-even rates. An unspent copy isn't saved for later — the next re-prepare does nothing, so the copy is simply deleted. This is the most common mistake this format will produce.
Hold instant removal for their upkeep. Killing a prepared creature before it casts the copy is a clean 2-for-1 in your favour. Which means instant-speed removal is better than usual here and sorcery-speed is worse — bump instants half a grade.
As the prepared player, cast the copy at the first reasonable window. Hoarding it for a perfect line loses you a card to a removal spell you could see coming.

Heartwood & threshold

Heartwood

A red-and-green artifact token that taps for {R} or {G} — and nothing else. In Limited a mana rock is card disadvantage that buys a turn, only worth it with a real top end. It pays for an R or G splash specifically; it does nothing for a W, U or B splash. The set's actual fixing is ten common dual lands, one for every pair — see the 3 Colours tab, which has been corrected.

Threshold

Seven or more cards in your graveyard — reachable turn 5–6 with Theorix self-mill, turn 9+ without. That gap means threshold cards are U/B cards, full stop. Everywhere else, read the threshold text as absent. The upside: they always wheel to the deck that wants them.

Where the best commons will be

  • Unconditional instant-speed removal at ≤3 mana, in any colour — expect black and red.
  • The Fatehold prepared commons — a body, a 2/2 and a surveil at common is a broken rate, and there appear to be at least three of them.
  • The Vigorbloom prepared commons — counters and life on a body.
  • Any common with empower Jace 3 (Theorist's Proxy is the confirmed example).
  • Common flyers in the 2–4 slot — this format stalls, and evasion breaks stalls.

Planeswalker note

FRA has more planeswalkers than any premier set since War of the Spark, mostly via Jace tokens but not only. Two consequences in Limited: an unanswered walker ends games, and your removal should be able to point at a planeswalker — value burn and evasive creatures a little higher than usual.

The real gate: the planeswalker clause

All ten common duals share one line — enters tapped unless you control a planeswalker. That is the actual constraint in this format, and it is not a colour-wheel question, it's a deck question.

A planeswalker arriving the same turn does not count. Playing the dual on the turn you make your first Jace does not untap it. The Jace has to already be there.
Your duals are taplands on turns 1–3 and real duals from turn 4. Backwards for an aggro deck, fine for a midrange one. Three colours here is a midrange plan — R/W Ajani's Army should almost never do it.
Empower Jace is this set's mana-fixing mechanic. It appears at common in every colour, which is why it is in every colour. A deck with six empower cards has six duals that work; a deck with one has six taplands. Want 3+ empower cards before committing to a third colour.
The honest rule, restated: two duals is the price of entry for a third colour, and a planeswalker you can land by turn 3 is the price of the duals. With two duals and the empower count, the third colour is genuinely open. With two duals and no empower, you have bought taplands.

The rule that survives

A dual sharing one colour with you is a free splash land.

If a land makes one of your main colours and your splash colour, it is never a dead draw. You aren't paying for the splash with a worse mana base — you're paying with a land that would have been fine anyway. That was true before and it is still true; it just now applies to every pair rather than five of them. Pick your pair to see all three third colours and the two duals each one brings:

When to splash

Splash for

  • Bombs — an Elder Sphinx, a planeswalker
  • Premium removal
  • Expensive cards you'll cast on turn 6 anyway — by then you've drawn your sources

Never splash for

  • Two-drops
  • Curve filler
  • Synergy pieces that must arrive on time

A card you can't cast on curve isn't doing its job.

Single coloured pip → 2–3 sources. Double pip → that's a main colour, not a splash. {1}{R} and {R}{R} are the same colour and completely different cards. Count a dual as a source for both its colours, but discount it on turns 1–3 unless your empower count is high.
Room of Refuge is the splash land that asks nothing. Always tapped, but it needs no planeswalker and it makes any colour. In a deck splashing one bomb it is often better than the “correct” dual.

Draft vs sealed

Sealed

Much more likely. Deeper pools, slower games, more uncastable bombs. Count your duals and your empower cards before you settle on two colours.

Draft

You have to take them. Ten common duals across three packs means fixing is genuinely available — but it also means the player two seats down is splashing too. Take the second dual around pick 6–8 rather than hoping it wheels.

Sealed on Arena

Six boosters, 40 cards. Sealed is a different format from draft here, and the gap is wider than usual because Heartwood turns uncastable bombs into win conditions. Work in this order.

    The one number to change: if you're ramping with Heartwood into a real top end, play 18 lands. Sealed games in this format go long enough that flooding costs you less than missing your sixth land.
